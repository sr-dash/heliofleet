Positions and data

All positions come from JPL Horizons as geometric state vectors (no light-time or aberration correction) in the ICRF, referred to the J2000 ecliptic. Spacecraft far from Earth and the planets are stored as heliocentric vectors in au. The spacecraft around the Sun–Earth L1 point (SOHO, ACE, Wind, DSCOVR, IMAP, SOLAR-1 (SWFO-L1), Carruthers and, for the inset, Aditya-L1) and the Moon are stored as geocentric vectors in km; their heliocentric positions are Earth's position plus the geocentric vector.

Vectors are sampled on a uniform UT grid and stored as one file per body per year:

SeriesSample step
Parker Solar Probe3 hours
Aditya-L1 (geocentric, for the inset)2 hours
The Moon (geocentric)6 hours
SOHO, ACE, Wind, DSCOVR, IMAP, SOLAR-1 (SWFO-L1), Carruthers (geocentric)12 hours
Everything else: Earth, the planets, SDO, Solar Orbiter, BepiColombo, STEREO-A/B, MAVEN, Aditya-L11 day

Between samples the page interpolates each coordinate with a four-point Lagrange polynomial (cubic) on the two samples either side of the requested time.

A workflow refreshes the data on the first of each month. It refetches every year from the previous one onward, so predicted trajectories are replaced by fitted ones as missions publish them, and it appends new years when a mission's Horizons ephemeris is extended. Positions after a mission's last tracking data are planning predictions and can change by large amounts after manoeuvres.

Coordinate frames

Earth-fixed map (HEE)
Heliocentric Earth Ecliptic: the Sun at the centre, the x-axis toward Earth, z toward ecliptic north. Earth stays at the bottom of the map, so spacecraft move relative to the Sun–Earth line.
Inertial map (HAE)
The J2000 ecliptic frame of the data, fixed against the stars.
Stonyhurst (HEEQ)
Heliographic latitude from the Sun's rotation axis; longitude measured from the central meridian as seen from Earth (the projection of the Sun–Earth line on the solar equator).
Carrington
Heliographic longitude fixed to the rotating Sun, from the IAU rotation model: pole at right ascension 286.13° and declination 63.87°, prime meridian W = 84.176° + 14.1844° per day since J2000 (TT). As in SunPy, the Sun is taken as seen from Earth one Sun–Earth light time in the past.
Viewing geometry
For an observer at distance r from Sun centre: light time r/c, and its offset from Earth's (r − r⊕)/c, which is negative when the observer sees a solar event earlier; apparent solar radius arcsin(R☉/r), with R☉ = 695 700 km; B0 and the Stonyhurst longitude are the observer's own heliographic latitude and longitude. The disk-centre Carrington longitude L0 is computed with the observer's own light time, as CRLN_OBS in its FITS headers and SunPy's Carrington frame with that observer, so it differs from the Earth-based Carrington longitude in the Coordinates table by about 0.01° (14.18° per day) for each minute of light-time difference, 0.08° for Parker near perihelion. One arcsecond at disk centre spans (r − R☉) tan 1″ on the Sun, and features there look (r⊕ − R☉)/(r − R☉) times larger than from Earth.

These are the definitions used by SunPy, against which the page is checked (see Validation).

Derived quantities

Separation from Earth
The angle between a body and Earth as seen from the Sun.
Parker-spiral footpoint
The Stonyhurst longitude where the nominal spiral field line through the body meets the Sun, for a constant radial solar-wind speed v: φfoot = φ + Ω (r − R☉) / v, with Ω the sidereal Carrington rate (14.1844° per day) and r the body's distance. The spirals on the map use the same relation and ignore latitude, as is customary.
Speed
A central difference of the interpolated heliocentric position over ±10 minutes. For Parker at perihelion on 2024-12-24 this gives 190.7 km/s against 190.8 km/s from Horizons.
Light time and apparent Sun size
Light time is the body–Earth distance over the speed of light. The Sun's apparent diameter is 2 arcsin(R☉ / r), with R☉ = 695,700 km.
Spot visibility
A point on the photosphere is visible to an observer when the angle between its local vertical and the line of sight to the observer is below 90°.
Solar surface map and coverage
A surface point at Carrington longitude φ and latitude θ is visible from an observer at heliographic longitude L, latitude B and distance r when sin θ sin B + cos θ cos B cos(φ − L) > R☉ / r; the left side of that inequality is μ, the cosine of the angle from the observer's disc centre. Each observer's limb is the circle where the two sides are equal, drawn on the map; Earth's limb separates the near side from the far side. Only observers that carry a solar-disc imager are shown; Earth stands for SDO, SOHO, Aditya-L1 and ground observatories. Pointing at another observer's limb or disc centre greys out that observer's far side instead of Earth's. The rings are the Parker-spiral footpoints described above, plotted at each spacecraft's heliographic latitude. The equator statistics below the map use the same test in 1° bins, with "well placed" meaning μ > 0.5 (within 60° of disc centre).
Synoptic magnetograms
The background is the SDO/HMI radial-field synoptic map for the Carrington rotation that contains the chosen moment (hmi.Synoptic_Mr_small, from JSOC), and the daily-updated synoptic frame for the rotation in progress. Maps are kept on HMI's native grid, uniform in Carrington longitude and sine latitude, averaged 2 × 2 to 1° in longitude and 1/90 in sine latitude, and clipped to ±20 G, the fixed colour range (matplotlib's bwr). For display only, the map is smoothed with a Gaussian of about one grid cell (σ ≈ 1° in longitude), ignoring cells without data, and drawn at 55% opacity; the readout under the pointer gives the unsmoothed value. A synoptic map assembles each longitude from observations near central meridian over the rotation, so it is not a snapshot of the Sun at the chosen moment, and the far side shows field observed up to two weeks earlier or later. Dates after the latest daily frame, before HMI's first rotation map (CR 2096, April 2010) and in rotations JSOC does not publish (CR 2119–2127) are left blank.

Events and searches

Alignment search (research tools)
Steps through the chosen range every 6 hours (every hour when Parker is involved) and reports runs within the tolerance: radial alignment when the Sun-centred angle between two bodies is within 5° by default; quadrature when a spacecraft is 90° ± 5° from Earth as seen from the Sun; magnetic connection when the two footpoint longitudes are within 10° at the chosen wind speed.
Calendar of upcoming events
Built monthly on a 3-hour grid: perihelia of Parker and Solar Orbiter (from the stored samples, as minima within ±20 days), radial alignments within 3°, quadratures within 1°, and solar conjunctions when a spacecraft beyond the Sun is within 2° of Sun centre as seen from Earth. Eclipse times are from the NASA eclipse catalogue.
CME fronts
From the NASA CCMC DONKI cone-model analyses: each CME leaves 21.5 R☉ at the analysed time and moves at the analysed speed, in the analysed direction and half-width, at constant speed. The front is drawn until it passes 2.2 au. No drag or deceleration is modelled.

Instrument fields of view

On the map, each coronagraph and heliospheric imager is drawn in the ecliptic plane as the range of elongation (angle from Sun centre, seen from the spacecraft) that it covers. Coronagraph limits given in solar radii are converted to elongation at the spacecraft's current distance. Coronagraphs are centred on the Sun; STEREO HI looks toward the Earth side of the Sun, SoloHI to solar east, and WISPR along Parker's direction of travel.

Proba-3, GOES-19 and PUNCH are in Earth orbit and are drawn from Earth's position, which makes no visible difference at coronal scales. Each instrument is drawn only from its launch date. Proba-3 ASPIICS is not drawn from 14 February to 4 June 2026, while the Coronagraph spacecraft was out of contact and the pair out of formation. ASPIICS has a square field reaching 4.2 R☉ in its corners and PUNCH WFI is three cameras round the Sun; both are drawn as annuli of the quoted range.

In two dimensions (the sky view), coronagraphs are annuli. Heliospheric imagers are modelled as fields of their published size: HI-1 a 20° square, HI-2 a 70° circle, SoloHI a 40° circle with the inner 5.4° removed, WISPR-I a 40° and WISPR-O a 58° square, both tilted 10° south. These outlines are approximate; roll angles and detector shapes are not modelled.

Sky view

The sky view draws the plane through Sun centre perpendicular to the line from the Sun to the chosen observer (Earth or a spacecraft). Solar north (the rotation axis projected on that plane) is up and west is to the right, as in a solar image; coordinates on the plane are in solar radii.

  • Each field of view is a grid of sky directions from its own spacecraft. Every direction is followed as a straight line of sight to where it crosses the observer's plane; the crossings are drawn as filled cells and outlined at the edges.
  • Earth and the spacecraft are placed where the observer sees them, in true perspective. A body more than 90° from the Sun in the observer's sky never crosses the plane in front of the observer; it gets an edge marker in its direction, labelled "behind".
  • MLSO rings and DKIST Cryo-NIRSP footprints are traced from Earth. Footprints are stored in helioprojective coordinates (Tx, Ty), which are converted to exact sky directions from Earth (Tx toward solar west, Ty toward solar north, measured from Sun centre). From another observer, they show where Earth's lines of sight cross that observer's plane, not where the light originated.
  • The Sun is drawn at the size the observer sees it: on the plane, the limb lies at 1 / √(1 − (R☉/r)²) solar radii.

Who sees this point. A clicked point is taken on the observer's plane of the sky. For each instrument the page follows the line of sight from its spacecraft to the point and reports the elongation, the equivalent distance from Sun centre in that instrument's sky, and the position angle measured from solar north toward east, as on a solar image. The point is hidden when the line of sight passes within one solar radius of Sun centre before reaching it. It is in an instrument's field when that line of sight lies in the field as drawn; a check confirms that every direction inside each drawn field passes this test. Cryo-NIRSP footprints are tested in helioprojective coordinates from Earth.

The plane of the sky is a construction for comparing fields of view: features are not confined to it, and a structure in front of or behind the plane appears elsewhere in each instrument's image.

Validation

On every change to the site, an automatic check (tests/validate.mjs) runs the page's own code against reference values from JPL Horizons and SunPy (scripts/make_reference.py). At the time of writing:

For scale, 0.001° of heliographic longitude at 1 au is about 2,600 km, and 1 au is about 150 million km. The larger interpolation errors come from daily samples of a spacecraft moving fast around a planet; they are invisible on the map.

A separate check (scripts/check_data.py) runs on every change and before each monthly update is committed. It looks for missing files, gaps, impossible jumps, out-of-range distances and ephemerides that have stopped advancing. The page's research tools also print SunPy code that reproduces any exported coordinates.

Known limits

  • Positions are geometric. Light time between a spacecraft and Earth is reported but not applied to positions; the Carrington longitude follows SunPy's Earth-observer convention.
  • Predicted trajectories, after each mission's last tracking data, are only as good as the mission's planning ephemeris.
  • STEREO-B has been out of contact since 2014-10-01 and MAVEN since 2025-12-06; their later positions are predictions, drawn faded and excluded from coverage and fields of view.
  • SDO is shown at Earth once its Horizons ephemeris ends, since it stays in geosynchronous orbit.
  • Heliospheric imager outlines are approximate (see Fields of view), and the side each one looks to follows a fixed rule rather than the actual spacecraft roll.
  • CME fronts use DONKI's constant-speed cone model and are a guide to where a CME was heading, not a forecast.
  • Daylight windows at Haleakalā and Mauna Loa use a low-precision solar position (about 0.01°) and say nothing about weather or observing schedules.
  • Cryo-NIRSP footprints are the spatial bounds listed in the DKIST inventory, as rectangles in helioprojective coordinates.
  • The magnetic field on the surface map is a synoptic reconstruction, not the field at the chosen moment, and is clipped at ±20 G; strong active-region fields saturate the colour scale.
